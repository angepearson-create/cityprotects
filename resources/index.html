01 — Right now

Helping Minneapolis now

These are the Twin Cities organizations carrying the work. Where it helps, a self-updating national directory is listed too, so you can find the same kind of help anywhere.

The one rule that matters most

Give cash, and give monthly. Unrestricted money moves faster than donated goods and lets an organization respond to what's actually needed. A small recurring gift is worth more than a one-time surge — it lets them plan and hold capacity between crises.

02 — Before the test

Building capacity where you live

If you don't live in Minneapolis, the most useful thing this book offers isn't a list to donate to — it's a way to prepare your own community. None of what protected Minneapolis was unique to it. All of it can be built anywhere, in calm weather, before it's needed.

  • Find out where your community stands

    Does your city or county have a separation ordinance limiting local law enforcement's cooperation with federal immigration enforcement? If not, ask your council why, and what passing one would take. The Advocates for Human Rights publishes plain-language guidance.

    theadvocatesforhumanrights.org
  • Build the interfaith relationships first

    The clergy coalition that showed up in Minneapolis existed because congregations had been in relationship for years. If your faith community isn't yet connected across lines — to Muslim, Jewish, and other communities near you — that work can begin now.

  • Find or start a mutual aid network

    The care networks that fed Minneapolis grew out of infrastructure built during the COVID years. Mutual Aid Hub maps existing networks and publishes guidance for starting one.

    mutualaidhub.org
  • Learn to observe

    Trained legal observers who document enforcement lawfully were central to what Minneapolis could later prove. The National Lawyers Guild trains community members; it takes a few hours and is offered in most cities.

    nlg.org
  • Know your rights, and share them

    The Immigrant Legal Resource Center's Red Cards explain, in plain language and many languages, what to do if immigration agents come to the door. They can be printed and handed out.

    ilrc.org/red-cards
  • Support your local legal aid

    Every community has one; every one is underfunded; each is the first line of defense when rights are violated. A small recurring gift helps it hold the capacity a crisis demands. Find yours through the national directory above.

    immigrationlawhelp.org
03 — If it comes

If a facility is proposed near you

Bookmark this before you need it. The federal detention build-out runs on speed and secrecy — the aim is to get a site approved before the surrounding community can organize, because once permits issue and construction begins, leverage collapses. Communities across the political spectrum have stopped proposed facilities anyway, by working the same points of leverage, roughly in this order.

  1. Act early and force transparency

    ICE rarely tells local governments before it moves. File public-records requests with your city, county, and state for permits, environmental reviews, and contracts tied to large warehouse purchases by federal agencies. Watch planning and zoning agendas.

    Tracking tools: Detention Watch Network · Indivisible's map of proposed & confirmed sites

  2. Show up where opposition becomes visible

    City councils, county boards, and zoning commissions are where opposition turns into something elected officials must answer to. In Surprise, Arizona, hundreds packed council meetings to demand the city force DHS to disclose its plans. What an official most needs to hear: constituents are watching, and it will matter at the next election.

  3. Use the local tools

    Zoning codes, building permits, utility-service decisions, and moratoriums all create friction. Jurisdictions have pursued environmental review under the National Environmental Policy Act, which gives opponents a legal foothold. Ask your city attorney or a local environmental-law group what tools you have.

    Legal resources on local authority: National Immigration Law Center

  4. Pressure the owners and contractors

    The private parties are not the federal government, and they answer to reputation and money in ways DHS doesn't. Warehouse owners have walked away from federal sales after sustained public campaigns. Find out who owns the site and who the operators are — the two largest, CoreCivic and GEO Group, are publicly traded, with boards and investors who can be reached.

  5. Build across the aisle

    The most effective opposition comes from places that don't fit a partisan script — fiscal conservatives who object to no-bid contracts and secrecy, environmental advocates, faith communities, and neighbors who simply don't want a federal detention center down the road. A coalition that crosses party lines is harder to dismiss and harder to caricature.

These aren't isolated flukes. In Kansas City, Oklahoma City, and Merrimack, New Hampshire, sustained pressure blocked proposed facilities. In Hutchins, Texas — a town of six thousand — the warehouse's owner backed out of a federal sale. In Social Circle, Georgia, the city cut off water service to a planned site until ICE explained itself. They're a pattern, and the pattern has a logic: apply it early, apply it together, and it works.

Build now what you will need then.

The strategy deployed in Minneapolis — concentrated enforcement in a pluralist city, meant to provoke the disorder that justifies escalation — is available to any administration willing to use it. What Minneapolis showed is that a community with strong civic institutions, deep mutual aid, interfaith trust, legal infrastructure, and the habit of documentation can absorb that pressure and remain recognizable to itself on the other side.

The distance between powerless and under-organized is not courage or virtue. It is preparation — the steady, unglamorous, often invisible work described here. It's the work that decides whether your community, when its test comes, will have what Minneapolis had.
